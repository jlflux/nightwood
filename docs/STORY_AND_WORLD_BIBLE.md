# NIGHTWOOD — Story & World Bible

**Story specification v1.0 — companion to the [Full Game Design Document](GAME_DESIGN_DOCUMENT.md).**  
**Spoilers throughout.** This file is for writers and developers, not an in-game codex.  
**Scope:** Canon for the initial narrative arc, with expansion hooks that are not required to implement the first prototype.

---

## 1. The narrative thesis

**Logline:** A 17-year-old student finds a sword hidden beneath his high school's auditorium and learns that the city he knows is built on a broken promise. He must fight monsters slipping through unseen cracks in reality while trying not to lose the ordinary friends and routines that made his life worth protecting.

**Theme:** Being a hero is less about being special than making room for other people in a life that already asks too much of you.

**Central conflict:** Eli has real obligations — class, family, friends, school projects, and the freedom to just play games — while secretly protecting Nightwood. The sword could make him powerful enough to rescue someone, but the secrecy could leave him completely alone.

**Tone:** Contemporary coming-of-age drama with escalating supernatural mystery and some humor. The arcade should feel like a real place to relax. The school should feel familiar and alive. Monsters should be unsettling rather than gory. The story can be emotional without relentless misery.

**Age and content:** Teen protagonist, teen friendships, stylized fantasy violence, mild frightening imagery, no gore, no sexualized minor characters. Romance is not required and shouldn't become the engine of the main plot.

---

## 2. Ordinary Nightwood

Nightwood is a fictional American city that got bigger by absorbing a few old industrial blocks and then never quite modernizing everything. There are renovated storefronts, a mall that is smaller than it used to be, sports fields, a lively library, bus lines, suburban streets, a creek that cuts through the older neighborhood, and a strange abundance of municipal power cabinets.

On the surface, Nightwood High is a perfectly ordinary public school: late buses, pep rallies, crowded lunch tables, sleepy teachers, repaired projectors, gossip, deadlines, and an auditorium no one has updated in decades.

The town's local folklore includes an odd story: occasionally, after the evening streetlights come on, somebody hears a **second school bell**. No one can agree where it comes from. It is always dismissed as a speaker malfunction, church bell, or memory playing tricks.

### Rules for writing ordinary locations

- Give every venue a genuine reason for people to be there outside the supernatural plot.
- Let conversations begin with ordinary concerns. A character should not speak in lore exposition just because the player enters a room.
- Keep recurring venues recognizable through day/night lighting changes; don't reskin every room as a dungeon.
- The library is not creepy by default. The arcade is fun. The basketball court is normal until the story gives it another meaning.
- No real school name, mascot, city or local student should be represented as Nightwood High. This setting is original fiction.

## 3. The hidden world — rules of the Veil

### 3.1 The Veil

The **Veil** is a thin, invisible boundary between ordinary space and a neighboring layer of reality in which patterns, memories, and sound leave physical impressions. The barrier has natural weak points, but Nightwood has more of them than it should.

The Veil does **not** rewrite all reality, send the player randomly through time, or make anything possible. It follows specific constraints:

1. **Breaches have anchors.** Something holds a breach open — a person, object, place, repeated sound, or power-network resonance. Removing or cutting the anchor can close it.
2. **Most people rationalize small disturbances.** Ordinary bystanders perceive flashes, strange noises, drafts, shadows, or a “skip” in time as mundane unless the disturbance is strong enough.
3. **Exposure is not mind control.** A person who directly witnesses a severe incident may remember it clearly, but others might disbelieve them.
4. **Strays need proximity to a breach.** They usually weaken away from their anchor and become more dangerous around it.
5. **Magic requires attention and effort.** Eli uses Focus, training, and techniques; the sword does not offer infinite effortless solutions.
6. **Bellwether can cut Veil anchors, not solve every social problem.** It cannot force someone to trust Eli, repair a friendship, or make an assignment disappear.
7. **No casual violence toward ordinary humans.** Bellwether's physical supernatural effect only manifests against Strays, breaches, and Veil-linked objects. This is both a story rule and a game rating/design constraint.

### 3.2 Why the second bell matters

For decades, an older municipal emergency sound network kept certain Veil anchor points stable. Its signature tone was incorporated into Nightwood High's mechanical bell system as a practical way to test the old relay. When the network is disrupted, the tone sometimes rebounds through the Veil and rings a **second time** — audible only to sensitive people or near a breach.

The sound itself is not a spell cast by the high school. It's a symptom of an old system failing.

### 3.3 The Strays

**Strays** are creatures or fragments drawn across the weakened boundary. Some behave like predators. Others appear lost or reactive. The player initially sees them as monsters, but later learns that not every Stray is malicious and some can be redirected or released instead of destroyed.

Visual grammar: sharp black silhouettes, hints of the environment they mimic, teal/violet edge distortion, and very clear wind-up animations. They should look strange even when rendered with tiny sprites.

### 3.4 The Chorus

**The Chorus** is a small secretive group that believes the Veil is a prison and Nightwood must stop suppressing it. They collect city plans, old recordings, and artifacts. Their members include both true believers and people trying to recover someone they lost.

Their core error is not merely “using magic is evil”; it is assuming they can choose consequences for everyone in the city.

### 3.5 The Custodians

**The Custodians** are an informal succession of people who monitor weak points, hide evidence, and perform quiet repairs. They have no official superhero headquarters. Their current knowledge is fragmented across library archives, municipal workers, and a handful of secret records. They are capable of real mistakes and disagree about whether secrecy is still justified.

## 4. Bellwether: origin, behavior, and limitations

The sword was made using metal from an old boundary anchor — a cracked bell clapper — and later reforged into a blade that can detect and cut the invisible lines connecting Strays to breaches.

**Design:** Narrow dark steel, forked guard, one luminous seam along the edge. At rest it folds to an ordinary scratched brass key on a school lanyard. Its first visual upgrade causes faint script-like notches to appear along the blade, rather than turning it into an enormous fantasy greatsword.

**Why Eli can wield it:** There is no bloodline prophecy. The sword responds to a person willing to cross a dangerous threshold to help someone else. Eli finds it while trying to rescue a missing student. At least two other people might have been able to awaken it under the same circumstances.

**What Bellwether cannot do:** Cut any ordinary door without consequence; kill a human; make Eli invisible at will; solve every puzzle; heal grievous wounds instantly; operate without the player's input; seal city-scale breaches by brute force.

**What it can do:** Harm and break the forms of Strays; reveal and cut anchors; channel Pulse and Ward techniques; resonate near a clue; act as a key to certain preexisting Veil seals; retain impressions from earlier wielders.

**Hidden truth:** It is not only a blade. It is a recording instrument. The earlier wielders left fragments inside it so a future protector could learn what the Custodians refused to write down. Some memories are incomplete, and some were deliberately edited by the people who recorded them.

**Narrative reveal sequence:** A strange sword → a blade that cuts Strays → a key to sealing breaches → a record of previous mistakes → proof that the old safety network was a compromise, not a permanent fix.

## 5. Main cast

### 5.1 Eli Ward — player character

**Age:** 17; junior at Nightwood High.  
**Home:** Modest residential area near the school's bus route.  
**Family:** Mother Nina Ward works emergency-care shifts; younger sibling Sam occasionally needs a ride or pickup.

Eli is reasonably bright, sometimes sarcastic, and not an instant expert. He has ordinary concerns about growing up in Nightwood and whether he will ever leave. His personality is expressed through player-selected responses: supportive, skeptical, deflecting humor, or careful honesty.

**Internal flaw:** He believes he can protect the people he loves by carrying every burden himself. The plot repeatedly shows the limits of that choice.

**Character growth:** From secrecy as reflex to learning to ask for help on his own terms. Getting stronger with Bellwether should run parallel to growing more willing to trust people.

**Writing guardrail:** Do not make Eli smug, invincible, or a perfect student. He should feel like a real teenager, not an adult detective in a school uniform.

### 5.2 Mara Ortiz — the investigator

**Age:** 17. **Role:** Editor and driving force of Nightwood High's student newspaper.

Mara notices patterns other people ignore. She wants a scholarship, has little patience for flimsy explanations, and considers unanswered questions an invitation to keep looking. She and Eli have history as classmates and intermittent collaborators, not necessarily romance.

**Initial belief:** Eli knows something about a missing student and is lying to protect himself.
**Arc:** Learns why Eli is secretive but insists he doesn't get to decide what she is allowed to risk. Can become a crucial ally or a complicated friend depending on player choices.
**Gameplay:** Evidence board, research quests, media skill tasks, optional article publication decisions.
**Voice:** Concise, perceptive, funny in a dry way.

### 5.3 Theo Brooks — the friend who remembers normal life

**Age:** 17. **Role:** Eli's close friend; loves arcade games and fixing electronics.

Theo helps make Nightwood a game worth living in. He knows the regulars at Quarterlight Arcade, is competitive without being cruel, and keeps inviting Eli to ordinary activities even as Eli grows distant.

**Initial belief:** Eli is stressed or hiding some embarrassing personal problem.
**Arc:** Starts with comic relief, becomes a person Eli may hurt through repeated evasions, then can emerge as a practical tech supporter once he learns enough.
**Gameplay:** Ghost Circuit scores, arcade challenges, Electronics unlocks, social choices.
**Voice:** Quick banter, occasionally surprisingly earnest.

### 5.4 Ms. Lena Park — the keeper of incomplete records

**Age:** Late 40s. **Role:** Archivist at Nightwood Public Library.

A thoughtful adult who knows more than she initially admits. She was not a teenage swordfighter; her role is preserving records the city wanted forgotten. She likes the library card club and treats students as capable of having real questions.

**Initial belief:** Eli's arrival with the old brass key indicates the safeguard has failed.
**Arc:** Moves from withholding information to teaching Eli how to evaluate evidence and challenge Custodian assumptions.
**Gameplay:** Research tiers, lore, Stacks 21 venue, clue translation.
**Voice:** Warm but precise; doesn't speak in riddles for no reason.

### 5.5 Nina Ward — family stakes

**Role:** Eli's mother, whose emergency-care shifts create genuine household pressure.

She is not a disposable hostage or a clueless obstacle. She trusts Eli with responsibilities, but notices exhaustion, unexplained injuries, and missed pickups. Her relationship provides a grounded reason why “saving the world” cannot be the only definition of being good.

**Gameplay:** Home scenes, small recurring choices, healing items, optional family errands.
**Voice:** Busy, affectionate, direct.

### 5.6 Sam Ward — younger sibling

**Age:** 12–13.  
**Role:** A small number of authored scenes that reveal Eli's protective instinct, especially when ordinary family plans conflict with danger. Sam has hobbies and their own life; they are not written only to be endangered.

### 5.7 Dr. Cora Hayes — principal

**Role:** Administrator trying to keep a school operating through rising disturbances.

Her suspicion has mundane explanations. She knows the building has a history of electrical issues and missing maintenance records; she does not initially know the full Veil truth. Exposure affects how she reacts to Eli's lateness, restricted doors, and odd behavior.

**Arc:** She may become a procedural obstacle or a reluctant source of help, depending on evidence and player choices.

### 5.8 Calder Reese — civic contractor / secondary antagonist

**Role:** Leads a citywide modernization and emergency-network replacement contract.

Reese is competent, generous in public, and too determined to dismantle an old system he considers obsolete. Years earlier, he lost someone in a Veil-related incident and believes a permanently opened boundary could bring them back. The Chorus exploits his grief and access.

**Important nuance:** He is responsible for dangerous choices, but he didn't invent every problem and is not a cackling villain. His actions remain morally consequential even when the player understands him.

### 5.9 Vera Kell — the Chorus leader

Former Custodian apprentice who thinks the old rules are another form of violence: suppression, memory erasure, and unacknowledged losses. She sees Eli's sword as a key to tearing down the network for good.

**Story function:** The person who understands Bellwether most clearly and chooses the wrong solution for reasons that still make sense.

## 6. Four-act story outline

### ACT I — After the Second Bell

**Beginning:** A school-day prologue demonstrates Eli's friends and choices. Players can walk around campus, complete a small modern-skill task, and play at least one mini-game before the first major combat event.

**Inciting event:** After an extracurricular activity, a power outage interrupts the school. Nora Kim, a younger student, disappears near the auditorium. Eli follows a sound that should be impossible — the school bell ringing after hours, then ringing *again*.

**Sword discovery:** In a sealed maintenance recess under the auditorium stage, Eli finds a scratched brass key and a wrapped metal object among labeled stage props. The object unfolds into Bellwether when a Stray corners him. Eli doesn't know what to do, but manages to fight it off and create a path to safety.

**First mystery:** Nora's phone, left behind, contains a corrupted audio clip of the second bell and a message that appears to have been sent from somewhere beneath the school.

**End of act:** Eli discovers the missing student is alive, but trapped across the Veil. He promises not to let her disappear in a school incident report. Ms. Park reacts to the brass key before Eli tells her what it is.

**Player emotional beat:** The first battle is frightening, but the more uncomfortable moment is getting a normal text from Theo asking whether Eli is coming to the arcade.

### ACT II — The Town That Doesn't Notice

**Expansion:** Breaches appear around the arcade wiring, a creek pump station, the library's old recordings, and the athletics complex. Each site serves a mundane activity and a supernatural clue.

**Investigation:** The second bell was part of an old city signal designed to stabilize a set of weak points. New infrastructure upgrades are removing dampers and feeding energy into those same anchors.

**Friendship tension:** Mara finds discrepancies in old construction plans. Theo catches Eli leaving a supposedly closed tunnel. Nina notices Eli avoiding home.

**Midpoint reversal:** The monsters are not all invading predators. Some Strays are fragments caught by the city's protective network. Eli may be fighting symptoms rather than causes.

**End of act:** Eli saves Nora from a small Veil pocket, but she returns with memories no one can explain. One of her drawings maps the deepest anchor directly beneath Nightwood High.

**Player emotional beat:** Rescuing Nora should feel like a win, but the town's polite refusal to acknowledge what happened is almost worse than the fight.

### ACT III — The Price of Normal

**Escalation:** The Chorus accelerates the breach schedule and attempts to recruit Eli by showing him Bellwether's recorded memories. The Custodians admit their network sometimes trapped vulnerable people inside Veil pockets to protect everyone else.

**Personal fracture:** A missed family obligation and a school incident make Eli's secrecy costly. Mara demands the truth. Theo resents the distance. No friend is guaranteed to leave permanently; the outcomes depend on prior Trust and a genuine conversation.

**Antagonist revelation:** Reese has been removing old network anchors because he believes opening the Veil will allow him to recover someone he lost. Vera Kell encouraged the work but plans to use its final surge for a permanent opening.

**The blade's choice:** Eli learns Bellwether can cut the master anchor, but not without temporarily exposing the area. The safest moment will coincide with a crowded Nightwood High community event because the old signal equipment will be active.

**End of act:** Eli decides who to trust, which allies to ask for help, and whether school staff should be warned. The story remains playable even if relationships are strained; helpers or route challenges change.

**Player emotional beat:** Eli realizes protecting people *without involving them* can deny them any chance to help themselves.

### ACT IV — The Last Period

**Setup:** A Friday evening school/community event around the athletics complex fills the campus. Reused school zones look alive with silhouettes, lighting, announcements, and moving NPC groups, rather than requiring hundreds of unique animated people.

**Crisis:** The second bell sounds over the PA. Several small breaches open across familiar areas. The final chapter alternates short combat encounters with evacuations, support from allies, and a showdown beneath the auditorium/old signal room.

**Antagonist confrontation:** Vera attempts to turn Bellwether into a permanent key while Reese finally understands the city cannot safely absorb the uncontrolled Veil. Eli can defeat them and seal the immediate breach, but the larger truth cannot be put back exactly as it was.

**Resolution options:** The player may choose **Quiet Repair** (maintain a safer, repaired boundary while continuing careful secrecy) or **Truth in Pieces** (share verifiable portions of events with trusted adults and friends). These are tonal/world-state choices, not a requirement to build two separate games. Both save the school and allow future stories.

**Ending image:** The next morning, campus looks nearly normal. Eli walks past the arcade and library, receives a message from a friend, and hears a single ordinary bell. Bellwether's seam briefly glows — Nightwood isn't finished with him.

**Future hook:** Somewhere beyond city limits, a recording of that second bell plays from a device that isn't connected to Nightwood's network.

## 7. Detailed opening scene plan (buildable vertical slice)

The opening should be **playable within 30–60 seconds**, not a five-minute cinematic. Approximate sequence:

### Scene 1 — Hallway, daytime (2–4 minutes)

- Establish player movement and interaction with two NPCs.
- Mara asks whether Eli saw Nora near the auditorium after rehearsal.
- Theo invites Eli to beat his Ghost Circuit score at Quarterlight.
- Player chooses one of two small tasks:
  - Help with a broken media-center terminal (Electronics XP), or
  - Help Mara compare student-paper notes (Research XP).
- Show the selected skill gain immediately.

### Scene 2 — Freedom to visit (3–8 minutes optional)

- Walk to the arcade/library nearby or select a clearly marked quick-visit option after the venue is discovered.
- Play Ghost Circuit or Stacks 21; both result in a score and independent skill progression.
- A text from Theo or Mara gently points toward the auditorium story when the player is ready.

### Scene 3 — The wrong bell (2–4 minutes)

- Nightwood High lights flicker after an extracurricular event.
- A distorted bell sounds once, then again after a beat.
- Nora's phone is found on a stairwell. The environment tells the player where to go without a large quest log.
- A door under the stage is ajar. Entering triggers a short authored scene.

### Scene 4 — Bellwether (4–7 minutes)

- Eli reaches for a strange key/metal object in an old cabinet.
- It unfolds into the sword as a Strayling blocks his exit.
- Interactive tutorial: move → sword strike → dodge → Pulse; one instruction at a time, each completed by play.
- Player defeats two brief enemies and escapes through a narrow utility passage.
- The sword folds back into the brass key as fluorescent lights return.

### Scene 5 — Consequence (1–3 minutes)

- Outside, Theo asks where Eli went. The player chooses an honest-but-partial answer or a deflection.
- Trust changes slightly; quest still continues in either case.
- Ms. Park, contacted through a clue found with Nora's phone, recognizes the key.
- Chapter objective appears: **Find out why the bell rang twice.**
- Auto-save and free-play open, with the arcade/library still immediately available.

**Vertical slice does not need all Act I content.** It needs to end at a compelling “I want to keep playing” moment.

## 8. Quest sketches

### 8.1 “The Article”

Mara investigates minor electrical events at school. Research uncovers a historical issue; Media can gather a photo; an optional Electronics interaction tests a damaged meter. Eli chooses how much proof to share. Reward: Research/Media XP, a relationship adjustment, and a lead to the service tunnels.

### 8.2 “Cabinet No. 4”

Theo has an arcade cabinet resetting at exactly the same point every evening. It begins as an ordinary troubleshooting task and optional Ghost Circuit challenge. Later, the cabinet's speaker reproduces the second bell's frequency. Players who focused on Gaming get special dialogue and an alternative lead; others can still find the clue through ordinary inspection.

### 8.3 “Overdue”

Ms. Park asks Eli to return a misfiled city engineering archive. Players can perform a short card game, browse records, or solve a simple shelf-location puzzle. Beneath the archive is a diagram of the school's old signal network. Noncombat play opens the same investigative path as a creek-side encounter.

### 8.4 “Under the Bleachers”

A small Stray nest grows near the athletics field during an after-school event. Player can gather clues to weaken the breach or go into an accessible combat encounter. A prior Athletics milestone opens a faster route, but no level requirement blocks the mission.

### 8.5 “Home Before Midnight”

An optional family errand collides with an active breach report. There is no hidden timer. The decision explicitly tells the player which event they are committing to; both choices produce authored consequences and allow recovery. Eli cannot flawlessly satisfy everyone, but the player isn't secretly punished for pausing or logging out.

## 9. Dialogue principles and sample voice

**Do:**
- Start with everyday specifics (homework, bus routes, cabinet repair, reporters, errands).
- Use humor as a release valve after tense scenes.
- Give NPCs reasonable motives unrelated to Eli.
- Let adults occasionally be right and teens occasionally misjudge situations.
- Keep text concise enough for mobile, with optional deeper lore in records.

**Don't:**
- Make everyone speak like a fantasy oracle.
- Have every character repeat the game's mythology.
- Create unearned “chosen one” speeches.
- Make school life an endless list of obligations.
- Use lengthy noninteractive exposition to explain a mechanic before the player performs it.

### Sample short scene — after the first breach

**THEO:** You missed the machine resetting. Again. I'm starting to think it only does it when you're not here.

**ELI (choice):** “Maybe the cabinet hates me.” / “I got stuck at school.” / “Can I tell you something weird?”

**THEO:** That's three different answers, and none of them explains why your jacket smells like burnt pennies.

**ELI:** That's oddly specific.

**THEO:** I live a rich inner life.

A longer conversation can follow if the player chooses to confide in him. The goal is a recognizable friendship, not an exposition lecture.

## 10. Content production rules

### 10.1 Quest specification

Every authored quest should document: **ID; title; starting NPC/location; prerequisite flags; stages and triggers; player choices; alternative skill routes; rewards; failure/recovery behavior; dialogue beats; world-state changes; persistent save flags; and playtest criteria.**

### 10.2 Branch design

Keep choice outcomes tangible but manageable: different dialogue, trust/exposure, unlocked help, altered objective order, cosmetic scene dressing, changed epilogue. Do not promise hundreds of endings or divergent maps.

### 10.3 Environment storytelling

Each setting should show history in inexpensive ways: old maintenance labels, different dates on plans, scribbled locker notes, taped repair warnings, outdated arcade flyers, a library record with one page missing. These props produce lore at lower cost than cutscenes.

### 10.4 Continuity invariants

- Nightwood is the **city**, Nightwood High is the **school**.
- Protagonist is **Eli Ward** by default; his player-facing display name is editable.
- Sword name is **Bellwether**; disguised form is a **brass key on Eli's lanyard**.
- Hidden reality is the **Veil**; enemies are commonly called **Strays**; openings are **breaches**.
- The bell sounds **twice** when the stabilizing system misfires; don't portray every ordinary class bell as magic.
- **Mara** edits the student paper, **Theo** is the arcade friend, **Ms. Park** works at the public library.
- **Quarterlight Arcade** hosts **Ghost Circuit**; **Nightwood Public Library** hosts **Stacks 21**.
- Nora Kim is the missing student introduced in Act I and rescued during Act II.
- Bellwether is not a lethal weapon usable against human NPCs.
- Eli is not heroically destined because of ancestry; he earns the role through choices.
- The plot must retain room for ordinary recreation. Nightwood's world should not become nonstop horror.

## 11. Narrative roadmap / scope control

**Prototype:** The opening 5 scenes, Mara/Theo/Ms. Park introduction, 1 school consequence, a hint of Nora's disappearance, enough dialogue to establish personality. All deeper mysteries remain implied.

**First release:** Act I and substantial Act II, recurring relationships, completed opening city mystery with a satisfying midgame resolution. Do not cut off the game immediately after the sword tutorial.

**Future story release:** Acts III–IV, the full Chorus/Custodian conflict, restored versus exposed boundary ending, epilogue variants.

This narrative arc is a planned creative direction, not a promise that all acts are available in the first public demo.

**Writer handoff rule:** Preserve the tension between saving Nightwood and living in it. Every major supernatural sequence should have a recognizable ordinary-life echo — a person to tell, a commitment delayed, a familiar place changed, or a quiet moment afterward.
